Project 3: Image Warping & Mosaicing

CS180 • Fall 2025

Overview

I assemble panoramas from overlapping photos. The pipeline is simple in theory and picky in practice: estimate a homography from point pairs, resample with inverse mapping, and blend the overlaps so that transitions are invisible. The two failure modes are geometric (bad \(H\)) and photometric (hard seams). I explore weighting strategies (Hann, edge-distance, distance-transform) and a compact Laplacian pyramid to hide both sharp and low-frequency seams.
Homographies are perfect for planar scenes or roughly distant content. Once the geometry is right, the remaining issues are mostly about how intensity and detail should “hand off” between images—i.e., a problem of where and at which scale to fade.

A.1 — Input Photos

Three scenes, each with left, center, and right frames (≈40–70% overlap). Camera rotated about one point to minimize parallax. The center frame defines the canvas; side frames are projected into this coordinate system.
Practical tips: avoid strong lens distortion, shoot quickly to keep lighting stable, and spread your clicked points over the whole image so the linear system for \(H\) isn’t ill-conditioned.

A.2 — Estimating Homographies

With point correspondences \((x,y)\leftrightarrow(x',y')\) we solve for \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\) (8 dof; fix \(H_{33}=1\)). I use the normalized DLT and least squares over ≥10 pairs. Reporting reprojection RMSE per pair makes it easy to spot a mislabeled click or weak configuration.
Sound heuristics: distribute points widely, avoid nearly collinear sets, and prefer corners/edges. The homography itself is not the goal—it is a tool to make resampling well-posed.
pairs: ihouse, stadium_low, stadium_high

A.3 — Inverse Mapping & Sampling

I treat pixel centers as the sampling grid. For each canvas location \(\mathbf{x}_c\) I compute \(\mathbf{x}_s \sim H^{-1}\mathbf{x}_c\) and read the source. This guarantees a value for every canvas pixel and avoids gaps. Two hand-rolled interpolants are used:
  • Nearest neighbor: pick the closest integer coordinate. It preserves exact source values but produces stair-steps along slanted edges.
  • Bilinear: blend four neighbors by their subpixel offsets. It’s still cheap, suppresses jaggies, and slightly smooths noise and high-frequency detail.
I also track a binary valid mask during warping. Later, when multiple warped images overlap, weights are normalized per pixel so that the contributions sum to one.

A.3 — Rectification Results & Timings

Rectification is a unit test for everything above: select the four corners of a planar patch (order: TL→TR→BR→BL), map that quadrilateral to a fronto-parallel rectangle, and render with both interpolants. A.4 — Blending into a Mosaic

After alignment, the main question is how to share pixels in overlaps. I tried three single-scale weightings and one multi-scale variant:
  • Hann feather: analytic falloff to zero near the image borders—simple and fast.
  • Edge-distance: weights grow with distance to the rectangular bounds; broader, geometry-aware ramps.
  • Distance transform inside footprint: compute a mask for each warped image and use the distance to the mask boundary (optionally blurred) as the weight; then normalize across images per pixel.
  • Laplacian pyramid: blend at several spatial scales with progressively blurred masks; hides both texture-level seams and slow exposure changes.

Key Takeaways

  • Rectification first: If a flat surface can’t be made rectangular, the mosaic won’t work either. It’s the quickest way to debug coordinate conventions and interpolation.
  • Per-pixel normalization is non-negotiable: never sum raw weights—normalize so contributions add to one exactly where any image is valid.
  • Weights encode trust: distance-based weights emphasize interior pixels (farther from cut edges) and tolerate small registration/exposure errors better than analytic windows.
  • Multi-scale saves stubborn seams: two to five pyramid levels remove both crisp edge seams and wide luminance steps without over-blurring.
  • Implementation details matter: work in float [0,1], sample at pixel centers, cache \(H^{-1}\), and keep masks. NN is great for speed; Bilinear is the default for deliverables.

Part B — Feature Matching for Autostitching

In Part B I automate correspondences for autostitching. The pipeline: (B.1) Harris + ANMS to get strong and well-distributed interest points, (B.2) descriptors from 40×40 windows downsampled to 8×8 with bias/gain normalization, followed by matching and RANSAC (next).

B.1 — Harris Corners & ANMS

Harris finds many candidate corners, but they can be highly clustered around textured regions. That is a problem for robust matching, because most points end up redundant. Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS) keeps strong responses while enforcing spatial spread, yielding a set of corners that is well distributed over the image.

B.2 — Feature Descriptor Extraction (40×40 → 8×8)

Implementation summary: for each ANMS point, I sample a 40×40 window (bilinear interpolation on a subpixel grid), lightly blur it, then downsample to 8×8. Each 8×8 descriptor is bias/gain-normalized (subtract mean, divide by std with ε-guard) to be robust to brightness/contrast. B.3 — Feature Matching (Lowe ratio test)

For matching I compare each descriptor in image 1 to all descriptors in image 2 using SSD distance in the 64-D space (the 8×8 patch flattened). I keep a pair only if it passes Lowe’s ratio test: the distance to the best neighbor must be significantly smaller than the distance to the second best neighbor. Intuitively, when the closest match is much better than the next alternative, the correspondence is distinctive and reliable; otherwise it is ambiguous and likely a false positive. I use a threshold around 0.5 (cf. Fig. 6b in the paper), which aggressively filters ambiguous matches.

The figures below show lines between the matched points for Left↔Center and Right↔Center. You can see that as we increase the number of displayed matches (e.g., going from a few top matches to many), more outliers appear—often visible as lines that cut diagonally across unrelated structures. In the next step (B.4) a RANSAC homography will prune those outliers by enforcing geometric consistency.
