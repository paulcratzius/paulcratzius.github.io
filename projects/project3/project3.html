Project 3: Image Warping & Mosaicing

CS180 • Fall 2025

Overview

The goal of this project is to turn a few partially-overlapping photographs into a single coherent panorama. Technically, we (1) estimate a homography between views so that one image can be projected into the coordinate system of another, (2) warp the pixels via inverse mapping with either nearest-neighbor or bilinear interpolation, and (3) blend overlapping regions so seams disappear. The hardest parts in practice are getting stable correspondences (homographies amplify small point errors) and designing per-pixel weights that gently fade one image into another. I compare three weighting strategies—analytic Hann, geometric “distance to border,” and a distance-transform (bwdist) inside the warped footprint—and also show a Laplacian pyramid blend that reduces high-frequency seams and small exposure differences across multiple scales.
Why this works: for planar scenes (or distant scenery), different photos are related by a projective transform. Once images agree geometrically, artifacts mainly live in the frequency domain (edges, textures, lighting). Good alpha design + multi-scale blending suppresses those artifacts where they occur (from fine detail to broad shading).

A.1 — Input Photos

I captured three scenes with a fixed center of projection (rotate in place) to encourage true projective relations. Each scene has left, center, and right views with roughly 40–70% overlap. The center image acts as the reference; the others are warped into the center’s coordinate frame before blending.
Shooting considerations: (i) avoid lens extremes that cause strong radial distortion; (ii) take the frames close in time to minimize lighting or subject changes; (iii) include textured, rigid features (corners, edges) so point selection is easy and homographies are well-conditioned; (iv) wide-ish FOV tends to make mosaics more dramatic.

A.2 — Recover Homographies

Given clicked correspondences \((x,y) \leftrightarrow (x',y')\), the homography \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\) maps homogeneous coordinates via \(\mathbf{p}' \sim H \mathbf{p}\) with 8 degrees of freedom (we set \(H_{33}=1\)). Using the DLT formulation, each match contributes two linear equations of the form p' × (H p) = 0, which we stack into a system \(A h = b\) for the 8 unknowns in \(h\). With only four points the solution is exact but fragile; I use ≥10 points and solve in the least-squares sense.
Stability tricks: (i) spread points over the image; (ii) avoid nearly collinear configurations; (iii) optional coordinate normalization (centering and scaling) improves conditioning; (iv) report reprojection RMSE to catch outliers.

A.3 — Inverse Warping & Interpolation

To synthesize a mosaic on a chosen canvas, I iterate over canvas pixels and map each one back into the source: \(\mathbf{x}_s \sim H^{-1}\mathbf{x}_c\). Sampling at non-integer locations requires interpolation:
  • Nearest neighbor: take the value of the closest pixel (fast, blocky on diagonals).
  • Bilinear: blend the four surrounding pixels proportionally to sub-pixel offsets (smooth; default here).
The output bounding box is determined by transforming the source corners and taking min/max in canvas space. Outside the warped footprint I keep a binary mask; it is crucial later for fair, per-pixel normalization of weights.

A.4 — Blending into a Mosaic

After registration, overlapping pixels must be combined without producing visible seams. I compare three single-scale alpha designs and one multi-scale method:
  • Hann feather (analytic): For each warped image \(I_i\), define a 2D weight that falls smoothly to 0 at its boundaries. A separable Hann is a common choice, e.g., \(w(x)=\tfrac12(1-\cos(2\pi t))\) with \(t\in[0,1]\) from center to edge, applied horizontally and vertically and multiplied. Fast and effective when alignment is tight.
  • dist (distance to borders): Compute the (normalized) Euclidean distance from each pixel to the rectangle’s edges (or bounding box in canvas). Far interior pixels get higher weight, edge pixels approach 0. This gives a geometry-aware alternative to Hann with slightly wider gentle ramps.
  • bwdist (distance transform inside footprint): Build a binary mask of each warped footprint in canvas space (1 inside, 0 outside). Run a distance transform D = bwdist(1−mask) so values grow with distance to the mask boundary, optionally blur \(D\) by a small \(\sigma\) to remove sharp ridges, then zero out outside the mask and normalize across images at each pixel: w_i = D_i / \sum_j D_j. This explicitly favors the centers of footprints and yields very soft seams in overlap zones—especially helpful when slight parallax or exposure differences remain.
  • Laplacian pyramid (multi-level): Decompose each input into Gaussian (low-pass) and Laplacian (band-pass) stacks. Build a Gaussian stack of the alpha mask and blend per level: L^*_{k} = M_k \cdot L^A_k + (1-M_k)\cdot L^B_k. Finally, reconstruct by summing all blended Laplacians plus the top Gaussian. Intuition: high-frequency seams (edges/texture) and low-frequency seams (lighting) live at different scales; blending each band with a suitably blurred mask hides both kinds of artifacts.
Below I show the final mosaics and the weight maps \(w_1,w_2,w_3\) (left/center/right) used in each scheme. Notice that bwdist weights get brightest near the center of each warped footprint and fade smoothly to 0 at the footprint’s edge, matching the “intuitive” confidence in pixels far from cut boundaries.

One Big Takeaway

Building a convincing mosaic is a two-part story. First, geometry: good correspondences and a stable homography keep structures aligned so you aren’t fighting ghosting. Second, photometry: an alpha strategy matched to the overlap and residual misalignment hides seams in both fine detail and broad shading. Distance-transform weights approximate “confidence” in the interior of each warped footprint, while Laplacian blending distributes the transition across spatial frequencies. Together they deliver clean panoramas even when conditions aren’t perfect.
